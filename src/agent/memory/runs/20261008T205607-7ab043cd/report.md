**Executive Summary**  
Attention-Deficit/Hyperactivity Disorder (ADHD) is a prevalent neurodevelopmental condition with complex etiology and significant impact on daily functioning across the lifespan. While symptoms such as inattention, impulsivity, and disorganization are well-documented, the disorder remains multifactorial in origin, involving genetic, neurobiological, and environmental influences. Current treatments include pharmacological interventions like stimulants and behavioral strategies, though gaps persist—particularly in adult-specific, evidence-based care and the integration of co-occurring mental health conditions. Despite advances, challenges in diagnosis, access to care, and public understanding continue to hinder effective management.

---

**Key Findings**  
1. ADHD is a multifactorial disorder influenced by genetic predispositions, brain structure and function, and environmental factors such as stress and diet [S1], [S14], [S5].  
2. Symptoms in adults commonly include difficulties with time management, organization, focus, and impulse control, which can impair personal, professional, and social functioning [S2], [S3], [S7].  
3. Effective management involves a combination of pharmacological treatments (e.g., stimulants), behavioral therapies, and self-care practices such as regular exercise and healthy routines [S2], [S6], [S8].  
4. Significant gaps exist in adult-specific treatments, co-occurring mental health conditions (e.g., anxiety, depression), and access to care—particularly in rural areas—limiting effective intervention [S4], [S5].  

---

**Supporting Evidence**  
- ADHD is described as a complex neurodevelopmental disorder with a multifactorial etiology involving genetic, epigenetic, and environmental factors [S1].  
- Adults with ADHD face persistent challenges such as disorganization, time management issues, and emotional overwhelm, which can affect relationships, employment, and overall well-being [S2], [S3], [S5].  
- Symptoms like inattention, hyperactivity, and impulsiveness are core features, with adults more likely to exhibit memory problems and restlessness than hyperactivity [S8].  
- First-line pharmacological treatments include stimulants such as methylphenidate and amphetamines, though evidence-based adult treatments remain underdeveloped [S1], [S15].  
- Co-occurring conditions such as anxiety, depression, and substance use disorders are common in adults with ADHD, emphasizing the need for integrated care [S5], [S4].  
- Diagnosis requires symptoms present before age 12 and persistent over at least six months, with challenges in adult recognition due to symptom evolution [S15].  

---

**Limitations**  
- The sources provided do not specify the exact genetic or neurobiological mechanisms linking ADHD to specific brain structures or pathways, limiting a mechanistic understanding [S1], [S5].  
- While evidence supports the effectiveness of current treatments, the data on adult-specific, evidence-based interventions is limited and often extrapolated from pediatric research [S4].  
- The sources lack detailed information on diagnostic accuracy, cultural variability, or longitudinal outcomes of treatment, which may affect clinical applicability.  
- No source provides a comprehensive analysis of how environmental factors (e.g., diet, stress) interact with genetic risk in a real-world context.
